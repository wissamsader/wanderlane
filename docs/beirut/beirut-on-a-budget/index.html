Beirut on a Budget: What It Actually Costs

Real hotel price bands, why cash is the actual currency here, and the honest gaps where we won't invent a number — so you know roughly where your money goes.

Beirut isn't cheap in the way some of its neighbors are, but it's a lot more affordable than a few heritage-luxury hotel names suggest — if you know which numbers are real and which ones we won't guess at. Here's what things actually cost, with the gaps left honest rather than invented.

Cash is the real currency, not the dollar sign on a menu

⚠ This is the one budget fact that matters most here

Beirut has run largely on cash and USD since the 2019–2020 banking crisis. Crisp, newer US bills are the practical currency for hotels, restaurants, taxis and tours; card acceptance is inconsistent outside upscale venues; and ATM withdrawals — especially in USD, especially on foreign cards — can be unreliable. The real budgeting move in Beirut isn't finding the cheapest menu, it's landing with enough small-denomination cash that you're never stuck negotiating a bad rate or a broken ATM mid-trip.

Eating without a fixed price list

We don't have confirmed per-dish or per-meal prices from this research pass, so we won't invent one here — but the honest shape of it is this: almost everything in our food guide is a cash-only, family-run counter, not a tourist-menu restaurant, and that's exactly the kind of place that tends to run cheap. Hamdi Al Zahabi Sweets is known locally for "generous staff and fair prices"; places like Abu Hassan, Malek al Foul and Falafel Tabbara have fed the same neighborhoods for 60-plus years on foul, hummus and falafel — not exactly luxury-margin food. The practical move is the same one locals make: eat where the queue is Lebanese, not where the menu has photos.

Getting around without wasting money

Each neighborhood — Hamra, Mar Mikhael, Gemmayzeh, Achrafieh, Badaro, Downtown — is walkable in itself, so you can skip transport costs for most of a single day in one place. Between neighborhoods, a short Bolt or taxi hop is normal, cheap and genuinely how residents get around, not a sign you've planned badly. We don't have a confirmed current fare for either, so budget for “a few short rides a day” rather than a specific number — full detail in our getting around guide.

☼ Agree the fare, every time

Official taxi counters inside the airport want you to agree the fare before you get in — and that habit is worth keeping for any unmetered ride anywhere in the city. Bolt's in-app meter sidesteps the need to haggle at all.

Free (or nearly free) things to do

  • The Corniche & Pigeon Rocks. A free 4.8km seafront walk out toward Raouché for a clifftop view of Beirut's only natural sea arches — best at golden hour.
  • Beirut Souks & Downtown. Free to walk the rebuilt streetscape around Martyrs' Square, next to the Mohammad Al-Amin Mosque.
  • The Bourj Hammoud food walk. Wandering the Armenian quarter's market streets costs nothing — you only spend when you sit down to eat.
  • Horsh Beirut. The city's largest green space, next to Badaro — free, though access and opening hours have historically been restricted at times, so check current status before counting on it.
⚠ Museums are self-ticketed, not free

The National Museum and Sursock Museum are both self-ticketed at the door rather than bookable online, and we don't have a confirmed current entry price for either from this research pass — see our things-to-do guide and budget a small cash amount for each rather than assuming they're free.

When to go to spend less

Winter is mild by regional standards and runs the cheapest hotel rates of the year alongside November and March. July–August is the opposite: the busiest and priciest season, as the diaspora comes home for summer and demand pushes hotel rates up across the board. April–June and September–October split the difference — warm and comfortable without summer's premium. Full seasonal detail in our best time to visit guide.

Spend it like a Beirut local

The cheapest version of this trip and the most authentic one are the same trip: a cash-only counter in Hamra or Bourj Hammoud instead of a Downtown tourist menu, a Bolt hop instead of a hired car and driver, and a self-guided wander down the Corniche instead of a paid tour. Landing with enough small, crisp USD bills does more for your budget here than any single discount — it's the one piece of prep that actually changes how every other price in this guide plays out.

Frequently asked questions

Is Beirut an expensive city to visit?

Not inherently — most of its best food is cash-only counter food that's run cheap for decades, and hotel options span from a simple Hamra budget stay to heritage-luxury addresses at £220–380. The real cost driver is timing: July–August pushes hotel prices up across the board, while winter, November and March are the cheapest.

What's the cheapest way to eat well in Beirut?

Eat where locals eat: cash-only, family-run counters like Abu Hassan, Malek al Foul or Falafel Tabbara rather than tourist-menu restaurants in Downtown. We don't have confirmed per-dish prices to quote, but these are decades-old neighborhood institutions, not high-margin restaurants — see our food guide for the full list.

How much does a hotel cost in Beirut?

A serviced suite in Hamra runs $70–90, a design hotel like The Smallville around $200, and heritage-luxury addresses like Hotel Albergo or the InterContinental Phoenicia run £220–380. We don't have a confirmed current rate for the cheapest budget tier (Hamra Urban Gardens) — check live listings directly before you book.
