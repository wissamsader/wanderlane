3 Days in Beirut: The Perfect Itinerary

Hamra breakfasts and the Corniche on Day 1, Beirut's museums and a Mar Mikhael night out on Day 2, then the classic Jeita–Byblos–Harissa day trip (or Bourj Hammoud) on Day 3.

Three days is a genuinely good amount of time in Beirut — enough to eat your way across two or three neighborhoods, see the essential museums and the seafront, and still have a full day left over for the classic trip north. This plan follows how the city actually sits on the map, not a checklist.

Day 1: Hamra, AUB & the Corniche

Start in Hamra, Ras Beirut's dense, bustling commercial spine and the most walkable base in the city. Breakfast is a foul-and-hummus counter, not a hotel buffet: Abu Hassan Hummus & Fool is open 24 hours if you're up early, or Malek al Foul — 'the King of Foul,' hand-mashing the same recipe since 1965 — a few streets over near the American University of Beirut.

Spend the late morning wandering Hamra itself: AUB's leafy campus, the secondhand bookshops, and a coffee stop at one of the cafés the neighborhood is known for. It's dense and a little frayed at the edges at almost any hour — that's the character, not a flaw.

Golden hour on the Corniche

Late afternoon, walk (or Bolt) down to the Corniche, Beirut's 4.8km seafront promenade — free, self-guided, and best at golden hour with the whole city out for its evening stroll. Follow it west toward Raouché for a clifftop view of the Pigeon Rocks, Lebanon's only natural sea arches, right as the light turns.

For dinner, stay in Hamra: Taht El Shajra ('Under the Tree') runs breakfast through dinner with Arabic coffee and inventive plates, or grab a manoushe from Abu Shadi — no sign on the street, reportedly the best in the neighborhood — if you want something lighter before an early night.

Day 2: museums by day, Mar Mikhael by night

Pick one museum for the morning rather than rushing both — they sit on opposite sides of the city. The Beirut National Museum in Badaro holds around 1,300 Phoenician-to-Mamluk artifacts, including the Byblos figurines, plus the quietly remarkable story of how staff hid and protected the collection through the civil war. The Sursock Museum in Achrafieh covers modern and contemporary art across 800-plus works, with the still-private, gated Sursock Palace visible right across the street. Both are self-ticketed at the door.

Early afternoon, head to Beirut Souks & Downtown around Martyrs' Square — the rebuilt heart of the city, 200-plus shops in a polished, walkable streetscape — and the blue-domed Mohammad Al-Amin Mosque next door, which stands beside the Maronite Cathedral of Saint George, a pairing locals point to as a symbol of the city's coexistence.

Evening: Mar Mikhael & Gemmayzeh

Cross town for the night to Mar Mikhael, Beirut's liveliest bar strip after dark, or one flight up the Saint Nicolas Stairs to Gemmayzeh's French-mandate townhouses and street art for a calmer version of the same energy. If you want the city's most talked-about table, LMATBAKH in Mar Mikhael runs a live chef's-counter tasting menu over two nightly seatings only — book ahead, it's dine-in only. Otherwise, the strip's grills and bars need no reservation at all.

Day 3: Jeita, Byblos & Harissa — or Bourj Hammoud

This is the day to leave the city, or to go deep into a part of it most visitors skip entirely. Both options fit comfortably into a single day.

Jeita Grotto, Byblos & Harissa The classic day trip

The single most popular excursion out of Beirut, within about an hour's drive: Jeita Grotto's two-level cave system (a former New7Wonders of Nature finalist), the Crusader-era harbor town of Byblos, and a cable-car ride up to the 'Our Lady of Lebanon' statue at Harissa for a view back down over the coast. Most operators run all three together as one full day.

Bourj Hammoud food walk Self-guided, in the city

A completely different Beirut: the Armenian quarter's dense market streets, basterma and soujouk shops, and Armenian churches, with no real equivalent elsewhere in the city. Time it around lunch at Basterma Mano (cured meats since 1966, the single most-reviewed business in our whole Beirut dataset) or Resto Ghazar, then wander.

☼ If you only have two days

Fold Day 1 and Day 2's daytime plans together — Hamra breakfast and the Corniche in the morning, one museum in the afternoon — and use your second day for either the Downtown/Mar Mikhael evening or the day trip north, not both. Something has to give; make it the museum you're less excited about.

⚠ Before you go

Lebanon carries formal government travel advisories that vary by country and change often, so this itinerary doesn't make an independent safety call. Some governments currently draw a real distinction between Beirut and Mount Lebanon — generally urging caution there — and the south and border areas, which carry stronger warnings; a cessation of hostilities announced in spring 2026 has held, though both the US and UK describe the situation as fluid. Check the current, dated advisory for your own country before you book, and again closer to your trip: travel.state.gov (US) or gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice (UK).

☼ When to go

April–June and September–October are the sweet spot — warm, dry and comfortable, good for both the city and the day trip north. July–August is hot and the busiest, priciest season, as the diaspora comes home for summer. Winter is mild by regional standards, with the cheapest rates alongside November and March.

Frequently asked questions

Is 3 days enough for Beirut?

Yes — three days covers Hamra and the Corniche, the essential museums, a night out in Mar Mikhael or Gemmayzeh, and either the Jeita–Byblos–Harissa day trip or an afternoon in Bourj Hammoud. Add a day if you want to do the day trip north and a full Armenian-quarter food walk rather than choosing between them.

What should I book before I arrive in Beirut?

Very little needs booking ahead — most of this itinerary is walk-up. The two exceptions: a table at LMATBAKH if you want Beirut's chef's-table flagship (dine-in only, two nightly seatings), and the Jeita Grotto–Byblos–Harissa day trip in the busy July–August season, when it's worth locking in a few days out.

Can you do the Jeita Grotto, Byblos and Harissa day trip without a car?

Yes — it's the single most popular excursion out of Beirut precisely because operators run all three stops together as one guided day, so you don't need to arrange your own driving between them. See our things-to-do guide for more on what each stop involves.
